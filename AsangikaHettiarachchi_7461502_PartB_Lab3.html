Part B: Predicting Medical Insurance Costs with Linear Regression¶

Problem statement¶

Use the Kaggle Medical Cost Personal Dataset to build a predictive system that estimates an individual's insurance charges. The workflow includes data preprocessing, exploratory data analysis (EDA), a 70% training / 30% testing split, linear regression, and evaluation.

Dataset: Kaggle – Medical Cost Personal Dataset

Download insurance.csv from Kaggle and place it in the data folder. The dataset contains demographic and lifestyle features such as age, sex, BMI, children, smoking status, and region. The target variable is charges.

4. Data preprocessing¶

The categorical columns (sex, smoker, and region) must be converted to numeric features before fitting linear regression. We use one-hot encoding and drop one category from each feature to avoid redundant dummy columns.

We also remove exact duplicate rows and separate the target (charges) from the predictors.

EDA observations¶

The dataset contains 1,338 records and seven columns. The inspection found no missing values and one exact duplicate, which was removed before modeling. The target variable, charges, is right-skewed: its mean ($13,270.42 before duplicate removal) is higher than its median ($9,382.03), and the maximum charge ($63,770.43) is much larger than the upper quartile.

The plots show that:

  • Insurance charges are generally higher for smokers than for non-smokers.
  • Charges tend to increase with age, although the relationship is not perfectly linear.
  • The charge distribution contains high-cost observations, which contribute to prediction error.

These patterns suggest that smoking status and age are important predictors, while a simple linear model may not capture every relationship in the data.

9. Evaluate model performance¶

We evaluate regression performance using:

  • MAE (Mean Absolute Error): average absolute difference between actual and predicted charges; lower is better.
  • RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error): gives larger errors more weight; lower is better.
  • R² (Coefficient of Determination): proportion of variation in test-set charges explained by the model; closer to 1 is better. It can be negative if the model performs worse than predicting the test-set mean.

These are regression metrics rather than classification accuracy, because insurance charges are continuous numerical values.

In [11]:
mae = mean_absolute_error(y_test, y_pred)
rmse = np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y_test, y_pred))
r2 = r2_score(y_test, y_pred)

print(f"MAE:  ${mae:,.2f}")
print(f"RMSE: ${rmse:,.2f}")
print(f"R²:    {r2:.4f}")

13. Conclusion¶

This project developed a Linear Regression model to predict individual medical insurance charges using the Kaggle Medical Cost Personal Dataset. The data was inspected, one exact duplicate was removed, and categorical variables (sex, smoker, and region) were converted into numerical features using one-hot encoding. The cleaned dataset was divided into 70% training data (935 records) and 30% testing data (402 records).

On the test set, the model achieved an MAE of $4,181.82**, an **RMSE of $6,240.21, and an R² score of 0.7724. This means the model explained approximately 77.24% of the variation in observed insurance charges. The MAE indicates that predictions differed from actual charges by about $4,182 on average. The higher RMSE reflects the influence of larger prediction errors.

The coefficient analysis identified smoker_yes as the largest positive coefficient ($22,874.45**), indicating that, holding the other included features constant, the model estimates substantially higher charges for smokers than for non-smokers. Age and BMI also had positive coefficients (**$251.25 and $328.38 per unit, respectively). These are model-based associations, not causal effects.

Overall, Linear Regression provides a useful baseline for predicting insurance costs, but its predictions are not exact. The actual-versus-predicted and residual plots should be considered alongside the metrics, particularly because the dataset includes high charges and may contain nonlinear relationships or interactions. Future work could test interaction terms (such as age and smoking status) or compare Linear Regression with nonlinear regression models.
